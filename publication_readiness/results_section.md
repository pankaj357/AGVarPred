# Results

## 1. Strong discrimination on a gene-held-out ClinVar test set

We first evaluated the frozen AGVarPred pipeline on the held-out ClinVar test set. The initial gene-level test split contained 16,175 variants in 923 genes that were not present in the training or calibration sets. After feature extraction, 12,385 of these variants were represented in the final feature matrices and were evaluated. Because the split was performed at the gene level, every evaluated test variant came from a gene that the model had never encountered during training, feature selection, hyperparameter optimization, or threshold selection. The model produced calibrated pathogenic probabilities and binary calls at the operating threshold of 0.42 selected on the scored calibration set. On this scored test set, AGVarPred achieved a receiver-operating-characteristic area under the curve (ROC-AUC) of 0.9753 (95% bootstrap confidence interval 0.9731–0.9775), a precision-recall AUC (PR-AUC) of 0.9606, an F1 score of 0.8945, a precision of 0.8952, a recall of 0.8938, and a Matthews correlation coefficient (MCC) of 0.8327. The Brier score was 0.0568 and the expected calibration error (ECE) was 0.0225, indicating that the predicted probabilities closely matched the observed pathogenic frequencies across the ten probability bins used for ECE calculation.

The strong discrimination on the ClinVar test set was observed consistently across molecular consequence classes after aligning predictions to features by variant_id. Per-class ROC-AUCs on the scored internal test set were 0.9842 for missense variants, 1.000 for stop-gained variants, 0.9983 for frameshift variants, 0.9997 for splice-region variants, 1.000 for UTR variants, 0.9995 for intronic variants, and 1.000 for non-coding exon variants. Synonymous variants in the test set contained no pathogenic cases (all 1,230 were benign), so ROC-AUC could not be computed; this reflects ClinVar ascertainment rather than model behavior. A large "other" category (7,352 variants, 3,668 pathogenic) had an AUC of 0.9413. The consistently high performance across evaluable consequence classes suggests that the model learned signals that distinguish pathogenic from benign variants across multiple variant types. A stratified dummy classifier achieved an ROC-AUC of 0.4959 on the same test set, confirming that the observed discrimination was not a consequence of class imbalance.

Calibration quality was also good on the scored internal test set. The calibration curve lay close to the diagonal across most probability bins, with only modest deviation in the highest-probability bin. The low Brier score and ECE indicate that the isotonic calibrator converted raw LightGBM scores into interpretable probabilities that approximate observed frequencies for ClinVar-like data. At the 0.42 threshold, the model achieved a recall of 0.894 and a specificity of approximately 0.94, reflecting a balanced operating point chosen to maximize F1 on the scored calibration set. Because the test set was held out at the gene level, these results provide an estimate of AGVarPred's ability to discriminate pathogenic from benign germline variants in genes that were not seen during training, within the limits of the scored subset and ClinVar's label distribution and ascertainment bias.

## 2. Variable generalization across independent external benchmarks

To assess performance beyond ClinVar, the frozen AGVarPred pipeline was applied to five independent benchmarks that satisfied the study's independence criteria: no gene overlap and no variant overlap with the training, calibration, or test sets, and labels derived from sources other than ClinVar. Performance varied substantially across benchmarks, reflecting differences in variant composition, curation stringency, class balance, and the relationship between benchmark labels and the signals captured by the model.

The VIP Database was excluded from this independent-benchmark set because all 1,075 of its pathogenic labels are derived from overlapping ClinVar annotations. Although VIP variants and genes are held out from the ClinVar splits, it therefore tests ClinVar-label consistency in held-out genes rather than generalization to an independent curation. VIP is reported separately as a consistency analysis (see §2.6).

**Figure 3** summarizes external benchmark performance and DeLong significance comparisons.

On the UniProt Humsavar benchmark, which contained 14,718 manually curated missense variants (1,317 pathogenic, 13,401 benign), AGVarPred scored all variants and achieved an ROC-AUC of 0.826 (95% CI 0.817–0.835), a PR-AUC of 0.275, and an MCC of 0.295 (95% CI 0.277–0.311). The modest PR-AUC and MCC reflect the low pathogenic prevalence (8.9%) and the missense-only composition of this benchmark, where protein-conservation-based competitors have a strong advantage. On the common subset scored by all four tools (4,631 variants), AGVarPred AUC was 0.833, compared with 0.900 for CADD, 0.883 for AlphaMissense, and 0.943 for REVEL. On the pairwise common subsets, REVEL achieved an AUC of 0.942 (14,379 variants), CADD 0.871 (14,716 variants), and AlphaMissense 0.882 (4,707 variants). These common-subset values show that AGVarPred is competitive but not superior to specialized missense predictors on the variants they can score.

On the Grimm et al. 2015 Selected benchmark, assembled specifically to avoid circularity with predictor training sets, AGVarPred achieved an ROC-AUC of 0.745 (95% CI 0.726–0.763), a PR-AUC of 0.188, and an MCC of 0.233 (95% CI 0.209–0.258) across 5,258 variants (512 pathogenic, 4,746 benign). Performance was lower than on ClinVar and DVD, partly because of the high class imbalance (9.7% pathogenic) and the broad, heterogeneous set of genes and variant sources included in this collection. AGVarPred outperformed REVEL on the jointly scored subset (AUC 0.746 versus 0.641 on 5,188 variants) and was comparable to CADD on the common subset (AUC 0.717 versus 0.695 on 3,819 variants).

On the DVD (Deafness Variation Database) benchmark, which contained 8,388 expert-curated variants (195 pathogenic, 8,193 benign) across 12 genes, AGVarPred achieved an ROC-AUC of 0.977 (95% CI 0.967–0.985), a PR-AUC of 0.788, and an MCC of 0.258 (95% CI 0.239–0.275). Although the MCC was modest due to the extreme class imbalance, the ROC-AUC was among the highest observed, and the model correctly classified most benign variants with high specificity. CADD achieved a higher AUC of 0.997 on its scored subset (8,279 variants), and REVEL achieved 0.979 on its subset (855 variants).

On the MAVE Independent benchmark, which contained 7,557 variants (3,751 pathogenic, 3,806 benign) across 25 genes with labels derived from functional assay score percentiles, AGVarPred achieved an ROC-AUC of 0.528 (95% CI 0.514–0.541), a PR-AUC of 0.511, and an MCC of 0.051 (95% CI 0.026–0.073). All compared tools performed poorly on this benchmark: CADD 0.562 (6,663 variants), AlphaMissense 0.697 (483 variants), and REVEL 0.699 (2,299 variants) on their respective scored subsets. These results do not indicate that the benchmark is flawed; rather, they show that molecular functional impact, as measured by these assays, is not equivalent to clinical pathogenicity. A variant may have a strong effect on a molecular assay without being classified as pathogenic in a clinical database, and vice versa. AGVarPred and the compared tools were trained or designed to predict clinical pathogenicity, so their near-random performance on a functional-assay-derived benchmark is informative but should not be interpreted as a failure of the benchmark itself.

Finally, on the gnomAD Common Benign benchmark, which contained 4,989 common benign variants with no pathogenic labels, AGVarPred achieved a false-positive rate of 0.0024 (95% CI 0.001–0.004) and an accuracy of 0.9976. In comparison, CADD had an FPR of 0.153 on 4,989 variants, AlphaMissense 0.070 on 516 variants, and REVEL 0.045 on 1,235 variants. This benchmark tests the model's behavior on genuinely benign population variation, and the extremely low FPR indicates that AGVarPred rarely misclassifies common benign variants as pathogenic. The no-allele-frequency version of AGVarPred, which lacks gnomAD AF, had a substantially higher FPR of 0.037 on this benchmark, underscoring the importance of allele-frequency information for recognizing benign common variation.

The variability in external performance highlights several factors that determine real-world behavior. Clinically curated benchmarks with explicit pathogenic and benign labels, such as DVD, yielded high ROC-AUCs, while benchmarks derived from functional assays or population frequency proxies, such as MAVE Independent and gnomAD Common Benign, tested different aspects of model behavior. Class imbalance strongly influenced PR-AUC and MCC: benchmarks with fewer than 10% pathogenic variants produced low PR-AUCs even when ROC-AUCs were moderate or high. These differences should be considered when interpreting benchmark rankings.

### 2.1 ClinVar-label consistency in held-out genes (VIP)

Although the VIP Database was excluded from the independent external-benchmark comparison, it remains informative as a consistency test. VIP contained 3,880 clinical-lab variants (1,075 pathogenic, 2,805 benign) across 904 genes held out from the ClinVar train/cal/test splits. All 1,075 pathogenic VIP labels were derived from overlapping ClinVar annotations (`eMerge_category='review'` + ClinVar Pathogenic/Likely pathogenic). AGVarPred achieved an ROC-AUC of 0.892 (95% CI 0.881–0.903), a PR-AUC of 0.753, and an MCC of 0.507 (95% CI 0.484–0.529) on VIP. CADD achieved a higher AUC of 0.927 on the common subset (3,862 variants), while REVEL was at 0.876 (1,715 variants) and AlphaMissense at 0.803 (153 variants). Because the pathogenic labels are ClinVar-derived, these results demonstrate that AGVarPred reproduces ClinVar classifications in held-out genes, not that it generalizes to an independent curation source.

## 3. Variant coverage distinguishes AGVarPred from existing predictors

A key practical distinction between AGVarPred and the compared tools is coverage of evaluated variants. Because AGVarPred is built on AlphaGenome functional scores, which are generated for SNVs, the model scored 100% of evaluated variants in every external benchmark and every molecular-consequence class evaluated. On the internal ClinVar test set, 12,385 of 16,175 split variants were represented in the final feature matrices and scored. By contrast, AlphaMissense and REVEL are restricted largely to missense variants, and their coverage dropped sharply for splice, synonymous, UTR, intronic, and regulatory variants.

On Humsavar, for example, AlphaMissense covered only 32.2% of missense variants, 28.8% of splice variants, 33.5% of UTR variants, 45.7% of intronic variants, 16.0% of synonymous variants, and 0% of stop-gained variants. REVEL covered 99.8% of missense variants but only 97.5% of splice variants, 97.8% of UTR variants, 96.1% of intronic variants, 44.0% of synonymous variants, and 0% of stop-gained variants. CADD covered most Humsavar variants but still missed a small fraction, and on Grimm2015 its coverage was only 72.6%.

The coverage gap was even wider on benchmarks enriched for non-missense variants. On DVD, AlphaMissense covered only 9.6% of missense variants and 1.7% of all variants overall. On gnomAD Common Benign, AlphaMissense covered 10.3% of variants overall but fewer than 6% of most non-missense classes. REVEL showed a similar pattern: high coverage for missense variants but very low coverage for non-missense classes. Even CADD, which is genome-wide in principle, covered only 72.6% of Grimm2015 variants and 98.7% of DVD variants, leaving some non-missense variants unscored.

This coverage difference has practical implications for variant interpretation. Variants that fall outside coding sequence, including splice-region, UTR, and intronic regulatory variants, are often invisible to missense-specialized predictors. AGVarPred's uniform coverage of evaluated variants means that it can provide a pathogenicity score for any variant in the evaluated set, including SNV consequence classes that existing tools cannot evaluate. Because coverage is itself a practical requirement—a tool that cannot score a variant cannot assist in its interpretation—the 100% coverage of AGVarPred on the evaluated benchmarks represents a meaningful advantage even when per-variant accuracy is similar to or lower than competitors on the subsets they can score. This advantage must be weighed against the higher accuracy of specialized tools on missense variants, as shown in the Humsavar comparisons, and against the fact that 3,790 of 16,175 test-split variants were not represented in the internal test-set feature matrices.

## 4. Performance on non-missense variants

To evaluate performance independently of coverage bias, we constructed a non-missense comparison in which AGVarPred and each competitor were evaluated only on variants that were not classified as missense, restricted to the subset that each competitor could score. Because competitor coverage of non-missense variants is limited, the comparison was performed within the AGVarPred-scored universe to avoid artifacts in which a tool with high performance on a tiny subset appears superior.

On Humsavar non-missense variants, AGVarPred (N = 5,901) achieved an ROC-AUC of 0.683 (95% CI 0.668–0.698), which was lower than CADD (0.849 on 5,899 variants), AlphaMissense (0.882 on 1,867 variants), and REVEL (0.935 on 5,583 variants). This result reflects the missense-centric optimization of the competing tools and the composition of the Humsavar non-missense set. It is an honest limitation: AGVarPred does not match specialized conservation-based tools on the missense-like variants that those tools were designed to score.

On DVD non-missense variants, AGVarPred (N = 7,663) achieved an ROC-AUC of 0.981 (95% CI 0.970–0.990), comparable to CADD (0.998 on 7,555 variants), AlphaMissense (0.979 on 73 variants), and REVEL (0.992 on 131 variants). AGVarPred's full coverage is particularly valuable here because the vast majority of DVD variants are non-missense, and the competitor tools could score only a small fraction of them.

On Grimm2015 non-missense variants, AGVarPred (N = 873) achieved an ROC-AUC of 0.752 (95% CI 0.708–0.791), outperforming CADD (0.731 on 593 variants) and REVEL (0.700 on 823 variants); AlphaMissense could not be evaluated because its non-missense subset contained no pathogenic variants. On MAVE Independent, all tools performed near-random on non-missense variants, consistent with the distinction between molecular function and clinical pathogenicity.

These results illustrate an important methodological point: common-subset and pairwise comparisons with AlphaMissense and REVEL are inherently biased toward missense variants because those tools cannot score most non-missense variants. When a comparison is restricted to variants scored by both AGVarPred and AlphaMissense, the resulting subset is enriched for missense variants and therefore favors a tool optimized for missense conservation. By evaluating all non-missense variants in the AGVarPred-scored universe, we remove this coverage bias and show that AGVarPred's non-missense performance is strong on the clinically curated DVD benchmark, even if it lags specialized tools on Humsavar.

## 5. Statistical comparison with existing predictors

Pairwise DeLong tests were performed on the common subsets of variants scored by both AGVarPred and each competitor, with Benjamini–Hochberg false-discovery-rate correction across the 11 valid pairwise DeLong tests. The results confirm the patterns observed in the full-coverage and non-missense analyses and should be interpreted together with the coverage differences described above.

On Humsavar, AGVarPred was significantly outperformed by all three competitors: versus CADD the AUC difference was −0.045 (q < 0.001), versus AlphaMissense −0.049 (q < 0.001), and versus REVEL −0.112 (q < 0.001). The large common subset sizes (14,716 for CADD, 4,707 for AlphaMissense, and 14,379 for REVEL) gave these comparisons high statistical power.

On MAVE Independent, AGVarPred was also significantly outperformed by all three competitors: versus CADD −0.033 (q < 0.001), versus AlphaMissense −0.161 (q < 0.001), and versus REVEL −0.199 (q < 0.001), although all AUCs were close to 0.5. The statistical significance here reflects the large sample sizes rather than clinically meaningful differences and is consistent with the functional-assay versus clinical-label distinction.

On DVD, AGVarPred was significantly outperformed by CADD (ΔAUC −0.022, q < 0.001) and REVEL (ΔAUC −0.076, q < 0.001), while the comparison with AlphaMissense was not significant (ΔAUC −0.263, q = 0.18) because the common subset contained only 143 variants.

On Grimm2015, AGVarPred significantly outperformed REVEL (ΔAUC +0.105, q < 0.001) and showed no significant difference from CADD (ΔAUC +0.022, q = 0.56). The comparison with AlphaMissense was not applicable because the jointly scored subset contained only benign variants.

The size of the common subset strongly influenced the statistical power of each comparison. On DVD, the common subset for AlphaMissense contained only 143 variants, so even a large observed AUC difference was not statistically significant after FDR correction. Subsets of this size are too small to reliably detect AUC differences, and their composition is strongly enriched for the small fraction of non-missense variants that AlphaMissense can score. This illustrates a general limitation of common-subset evaluation: it is informative only when competitor coverage is broad enough to produce representative subsets. Taken together, the DeLong tests show that AGVarPred is statistically inferior to the best missense-specialized tools on missense-rich benchmarks, competitive on benchmarks where non-missense variants are common, and limited by small common-subset sizes when comparing against tools with narrow coverage.

## 6. Decision curve analysis reveals prevalence-dependent utility

Decision-curve analysis was used to evaluate the potential utility of AGVarPred's probabilistic predictions across a range of decision thresholds. Net benefit quantifies the standardized utility of using the model at a given probability threshold, compared with the default strategies of treating all variants as pathogenic or treating none. On the scored internal ClinVar test set, which has 37% pathogenic prevalence (4,576 pathogenic and 7,809 benign variants), AGVarPred produced positive net benefit from a threshold of 0.01 through 0.99 and exceeded both the treat-all and treat-none reference strategies across the entire range. The maximum net benefit was 0.366 at a threshold of 0.01, and the integrated net benefit was 0.280.

On external benchmarks, the value of AGVarPred depended strongly on prevalence and class balance. On Humsavar, AGVarPred exceeded both reference strategies for thresholds from 0.01 to 0.20, with a maximum net benefit of 0.082. On Grimm2015, AGVarPred exceeded both reference strategies for thresholds from 0.02 to 0.17, with a maximum net benefit of 0.086, and on DVD it did so from 0.01 to 0.03, with a maximum net benefit of 0.015. On gnomAD Common Benign, the model produced near-zero net benefit by correctly classifying almost all benign variants, which is the optimal behavior for an all-benign benchmark. On MAVE Independent, AGVarPred produced positive net benefit from 0.01 to 0.53 but exceeded both reference strategies only from 0.49 to 0.53, consistent with the near-random discrimination observed for all tools on this benchmark.

When compared directly with AlphaMissense and REVEL on jointly scored subsets, AGVarPred's net-benefit curve was generally lower than REVEL's on Humsavar but higher than both on Grimm2015 for low-to-moderate thresholds. The integrated net benefit values were negative for AGVarPred on Humsavar (−0.239), Grimm2015 (−0.436), and DVD (−0.146), reflecting the narrow threshold ranges in which the model outperformed the reference strategies. CADD was excluded from the DCA comparison because its phred scores are not calibrated probabilities.

The narrower useful threshold ranges on external benchmarks reflect their lower pathogenic prevalence and the model's imperfect discrimination relative to the internal test set. For example, in a low-prevalence benchmark such as DVD, the treat-all strategy quickly accumulates a large false-positive penalty as the threshold decreases, so the range in which AGVarPred beats both references is narrow. In higher-prevalence clinically curated benchmarks, the useful range can extend to higher thresholds. The prevalence dependence of net benefit means that the same probabilistic score can be useful in one setting and not in another. We therefore interpret the DCA results as showing that AGVarPred has threshold-specific potential utility on some benchmarks, rather than broad clinical utility across diverse settings.

## 7. gnomAD allele frequency is the dominant predictor; AlphaGenome provides complementary functional signal

To understand which features the model relies on, we computed SHAP values for a random sample of 5,000 training-set variants using TreeExplainer. The dominant individual feature was gnomAD allele frequency, with a mean absolute SHAP value of 4.648, followed by the VEP IMPACT score (0.437). Among the AlphaGenome features, the highest contributions came from a CAGE assay in eye tissue (0.290), a splice-site-usage assay in ascending aorta (0.278), and a splice-site-usage assay in Purkinje cells (0.225). These top AlphaGenome features individually contributed less than both gnomAD AF and the VEP IMPACT score.

The dominance of gnomAD AF is expected: common variants are overwhelmingly benign, and rare or absent variants are enriched for pathogenicity. The model therefore uses allele frequency as its primary signal and supplements it with consequence severity and functional genomic information. The AlphaGenome features contribute a complementary layer that helps discriminate variants with similar allele frequencies and consequences.

When AlphaGenome features were aggregated by broad tissue group, brain/CNS features contributed the largest share of cumulative SHAP importance among AlphaGenome features (1.780 total, 22.0% of the AlphaGenome SHAP mass), followed by liver/digestive (1.593, 19.7%), heart/vasculature (0.792, 9.8%), blood/immune (0.677, 8.4%), reproductive (0.656, 8.1%), and embryonic/stem (0.640, 7.9%). The remaining tissue groups each contributed less than 5% of the AlphaGenome SHAP mass. The top two tissue groups had bootstrap 95% confidence intervals that did not overlap with zero (brain/CNS 1.397–2.263, liver/digestive 1.312–1.871), supporting that these aggregate contributions are robust within the selected feature panel.

At the biosample level, the largest contributors were HepG2 liver cells (8.47% of AlphaGenome SHAP mass), ascending aorta (4.37%), testis (3.95%), H1 human embryonic stem cells (3.69%), and camera-type eye (3.58%). The prominence of certain cell lines and tissues should not be interpreted as evidence that pathogenicity is tissue-specific in a causal sense. It likely reflects the availability of high-quality AlphaGenome tracks in the selected feature panel: HepG2, for example, is one of the most densely assayed cell lines. Other top biosamples included Purkinje cell (mean absolute SHAP 0.131 per feature), K562 blood cells (0.078), and dorsolateral prefrontal cortex (0.102). The bootstrap confidence intervals for the top biosamples were generally tight when multiple features were available, but single-feature biosamples had no interval estimate.

These SHAP-based tissue rankings are descriptive. They identify which tissues and cell lines contributed predictive signal within the selected panel, but they do not establish that pathogenicity is driven by specific tissues or that the model has learned causal tissue-specific biology.

## 8. Splicing and regulatory assays contribute the largest share of AlphaGenome signal

When the 111 AlphaGenome features were aggregated by assay type, histone ChIP-seq features contributed the largest single share (23.4% of the AlphaGenome SHAP mass), followed by splice-site usage (15.7%), TF ChIP-seq (13.5%), CAGE (13.1%), splice junctions (12.6%), DNase I hypersensitivity (8.3%), chromatin contact maps (8.2%), PRO-cap (2.2%), canonical splice sites (2.0%), and ATAC-seq (0.9%).

Splicing-related assays collectively accounted for approximately 30.3% of the AlphaGenome SHAP contribution (splice-site usage + splice junctions + canonical splice sites). Regulatory assays—including histone marks, transcription-factor binding, CAGE, DNase I hypersensitivity, ATAC-seq, and chromatin contacts—collectively accounted for approximately 67.5% of the AlphaGenome SHAP contribution. This pattern indicates that, among the AlphaGenome features, the model relies on epigenomic and transcriptomic regulatory signals rather than on protein-sequence conservation.

Individual top features reinforce this interpretation: the highest-ranked AlphaGenome features included splice-site-usage tracks from heart, brain, and muscle biosamples; CAGE tracks from eye, pancreas, and parotid gland; histone modification tracks from liver and blood cell lines; and chromatin contact maps from embryonic stem cells and fibroblasts. Among histone marks, H3K79me2, H3K36me3, and H4K20me1 features appeared in the top 50, suggesting that the model uses diverse chromatin states rather than a single histone modification. H3K9me3 features did not reach the top 50 in this sample. Among TF ChIP-seq features, POLR2A phosphorylated serine-5 and the splicing factor RBFOX2 were prominent, linking the regulatory signal to transcription and RNA processing.

The diversity of assays and biosamples represented among the top features suggests that the model integrates complementary regulatory signals within the AlphaGenome feature set. The strong contribution of splicing assays is consistent with the model's ability to score splice-region variants, while the broad regulatory contribution reflects the SNV-wide nature of the AlphaGenome score set. The relatively low contribution of ATAC-seq (<1% of AlphaGenome SHAP mass) may reflect the small number of ATAC-seq features selected or redundancy with DNase I hypersensitivity features, rather than a lack of biological relevance of chromatin accessibility in general.

It is important to reiterate that these assay rankings describe the selected feature panel and the model's use of it. They do not demonstrate that specific assays are causally important for pathogenicity, nor do they override the dominant contribution of gnomAD AF and VEP consequence to the overall prediction.

## 9. Performance varies by molecular consequence

Dissecting performance by variant consequence class revealed both strengths and limitations. On the scored internal ClinVar test set, ROC-AUCs were high for evaluable classes: missense 0.9842, stop-gained 1.000, frameshift 0.9983, splice 0.9997, UTR 1.000, intron 0.9995, and non-coding exon 1.000. Several of these estimates rested on very small negative sets (for example, only 3 negatives for stop-gained and 2 for frameshift), so the exact AUC values should be treated as illustrative rather than precise point estimates. The synonymous class contained no pathogenic variants, so AUC could not be computed; a large 'other' category (7,352 variants, 3,668 pathogenic) had an AUC of 0.9413. These internal results demonstrate that the model discriminated pathogenic from benign variants consistently across consequence classes within the scored ClinVar test set.

On external benchmarks, per-class performance was more variable and often constrained by small subgroup sizes. Estimates based on fewer than 100 pathogenic variants, fewer than 100 total variants, or fewer than 10 pathogenic variants should be regarded as exploratory and are flagged below. On Humsavar, which is almost entirely missense, the missense AUC was 0.911, while non-missense subtypes such as splice (0.939 on 198 variants, 16 pathogenic), UTR (0.842 on 182 variants, 8 pathogenic), and intron (0.900 on 359 variants, 11 pathogenic) were also reasonably well discriminated. The synonymous (25 variants, 3 pathogenic) and stop-gained (32 variants, 1 pathogenic) subgroups were too small for reliable estimation.

On DVD, the missense AUC was 0.939 (725 variants, 59 pathogenic) and the splice AUC was 0.930 (67 variants, 22 pathogenic), but the intronic subgroup contained only one pathogenic variant among 6,791 intronic benign variants and therefore produced an uninformative AUC of 1.0. The synonymous, stop-gained, and frameshift subgroups on DVD contained no benign variants or too few variants for stable estimation.

On Grimm2015, per-class AUCs ranged from 0.145 for synonymous variants (39 variants, 1 pathogenic; exploratory) to 0.746 for missense variants (4,385 variants, 409 pathogenic). The non-coding exon subgroup contained no pathogenic variants and could not be evaluated.

The MAVE Independent benchmark showed near-random performance across all consequence classes, with most AUCs between 0.43 and 0.57. For example, MAVE missense variants achieved an AUC of 0.568, synonymous variants 0.427, and "other" variants 0.532. These values reinforce the conclusion that functional assay scores in this benchmark do not align with clinical pathogenicity labels.

Across all benchmarks, the most reliable per-class results were for missense and splice variants, which had the largest sample sizes. Estimates for stop-gained, frameshift, synonymous, and intronic variants were often based on small numbers and should be treated as exploratory. We therefore emphasize the overall pattern of consistent cross-class discrimination on the internal ClinVar test set rather than any individual external subgroup AUC. AGVarPred's main practical advantage is the ability to provide a calibrated score for any SNV consequence class, including those that existing tools often cannot evaluate.
